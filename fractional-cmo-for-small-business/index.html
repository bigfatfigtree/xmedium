Straight answers

Does a small business need a fractional CMO?

A small business needs a fractional CMO when marketing has outgrown the owner’s spare time but not the budget for a full-time executive — for most companies, somewhere in the $1 million to $10 million revenue range. What you are buying is ownership: one plan, one person managing the vendors, and spend that gets accounted for. Published guides put typical retainers at $3,000 to $8,000 a month for companies this size. Run the fee test first: if the fee would eat most of your marketing budget, buy execution instead.

The ceiling on owner-led marketing

Owner-led marketing works until it doesn’t. The owner knows the customers and the offer better than anyone, so early marketing is good. Then the business grows, marketing becomes five jobs — the website, the ads, the social accounts, the agency, the occasional campaign — and every one of them gets the owner’s leftover attention. Decisions pile up. Vendors get briefed in fragments. Nobody can say what last quarter’s spend produced. The ceiling is not effort or talent. It is that marketing has become a leadership job and it is being done in the margins of a different job.

What changes when the seat is filled

Three things, and they are unglamorous. One plan: every channel and vendor works from the same priorities, set against revenue, not activity. Managed vendors: the agencies and freelancers you already pay get a senior person briefing them, reviewing the work, and cutting what is not performing — most small businesses get more from the vendors they have before they need new ones. Accounted spend: a monthly read on what went out, what came back, and what changes next. That includes local businesses. A regional firm, a multi-location service company, or a retailer lives on local demand, and local demand is exactly where uncoordinated spend leaks worst.

The sanity rule on fees

Be suspicious of any leadership hire, including us, until this math works. If your total marketing budget is $2,000 a month, spending most of it on strategy leaves nothing to execute with. That is backwards. Buy execution first — a good freelancer, a focused agency, one channel done properly — and add leadership when the spend is large enough that wasting it costs more than the fee. For most businesses that point arrives somewhere around $1 million in revenue. Below the leadership threshold, the honest answer is often a smaller engagement or none. The full cost picture, including what a full-time CMO runs, is in what a fractional CMO costs.

Asked often.

What size business is the right fit for a fractional CMO?

Published guides put the typical band at roughly $1 million to $25 million in revenue. Small businesses usually land between $1 million and $10 million: large enough that marketing spend is real money, small enough that a full-time CMO at $300,000 or more a year loaded makes no sense. Revenue is a guide, not a gate — the real test is whether a leadership gap exists.

Does a fractional CMO work for a local business?

Yes. Local businesses often run more marketing pieces than they realize — search, reviews, social, a website, maybe an agency — with nobody coordinating them. A fractional CMO sets the plan around how local customers actually choose, manages the vendors, and cuts the spend that is not producing calls, bookings, or sales.

We already have an agency. Would a fractional CMO replace it?

Usually not. Most engagements start by getting more out of the vendors already in place. The fractional CMO sits on your side of the table: setting the strategy the agency executes against, judging the work, and deciding what continues. If an agency is the wrong fit, you will hear that too, with the reasoning in plain terms.
