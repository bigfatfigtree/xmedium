Straight answers

Fractional CMO or full-time CMO: which is right for your company?

It comes down to workload and risk. A full-time CMO in the US commonly costs $300,000 to $570,000 a year all-in, and the hire takes months to make and a year to unwind if it is wrong. A fractional CMO gives you the same seniority for one to three days a week, at a published market range of $3,000 to $20,000 a month. If marketing genuinely needs an executive forty hours a week, hire full-time. Most companies under that load do not.

The cost math, honestly

Published 2026 guides put a full-time CMO’s total cost in the US at $300,000 to $570,000 a year once salary, bonus, benefits, and equity are counted, before recruiting fees. Fractional retainers in the same guides typically run $3,000 to $20,000 a month depending on cadence and scope, with most small and mid-sized companies landing between $3,000 and $8,000. That is not a rounding difference; it is a different budget category. The full breakdown lives in what a fractional CMO costs. Neither option is cheap. The question is which one matches the actual workload.

The risk is asymmetric

A full-time executive search takes months. Ramp-up takes more months. If the hire is wrong, you lose the better part of a year, plus severance, plus the team’s momentum, and then you start the search again. Companies also tend to hire the full-time CMO before they can describe the job, which is how the hire goes wrong. A fractional engagement is a contract: a 3 to 6 month initial term is typical, the cadence can be adjusted, and if the fit is wrong it ends without an org chart to rebuild. You can also use the fractional seat to define the full-time job before you hire it.

When each one wins

Hire full-time when the load is real: a marketing team large enough to need a daily executive, revenue past roughly $25M, a board and investor cadence that consumes the seat, and decisions that cannot wait two days. Hire fractional when the leadership gap is real but the load is part-time: strategy is missing, agencies are unled, the founder is the default marketer, and the budget cannot carry a $300,000-plus package without starving the work itself. Many companies run fractional for a year or two, then hire full-time once the function is built and the job is obvious.

Asked often.

Can a fractional CMO become our full-time CMO later?

Sometimes, and it is a clean way to hire: you have watched the person work for months before either side commits. It is not the default, though. Many fractional CMOs run a portfolio practice by choice. Treat the conversion as a possible outcome to discuss openly, not an assumption to build the engagement on.

How long do fractional engagements usually last?

Published guides describe a 3 to 6 month initial term as typical, with many engagements extending well past a year once the rhythm is working. Time to value is usually 30 to 45 days. If a provider asks for a long lock-in before any work has shipped, that is worth a hard question.

What if we already have a marketing manager?

That is the normal setup, not an obstacle. A fractional CMO gives your manager the senior direction, priorities, and cover they cannot give themselves, and usually makes them more effective rather than redundant. The arrangement to avoid is two people both claiming the strategy seat. One person owns the plan; everyone else owns pieces of it.
