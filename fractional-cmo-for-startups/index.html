Straight answers

Should a startup hire a fractional CMO?

A startup should hire a fractional CMO after it has a product customers already want, and not before. Past product-market fit, the problem stops being discovery and becomes direction: positioning, a go-to-market plan, the first marketing hires, and a story investors can repeat. A fractional CMO owns those calls a few days a week. Published guides put typical retainers at $3,000 to $20,000 a month, against $300,000 to $570,000 a year loaded for a full-time CMO. Before that fit exists, spend the money on customers, not leadership.

The moment it makes sense

The signals are consistent. Revenue is coming in, but from scattered efforts nobody planned. The founder is still the marketing department, and marketing is the job that gets done last, after product, hiring, and fundraising. Channels get tried one at a time with no system connecting them, and every agency or freelancer gets briefed by someone who is guessing. That is a leadership gap, not a labor gap. More hands will not fix it, because nobody senior is deciding what the hands should do, in what order, and what to stop doing.

What the seat does at a startup

Four things, mostly. Positioning: who the product is for and why it wins, said in words a buyer repeats. Go-to-market: which channels get tested first, with what budget, and what result kills or scales each one. First hires: the order matters — the wrong first marketing hire is an expensive year. And the investor narrative: the growth story in the deck has to match the numbers in the room. At Extra Medium the Lead practice pairs the seat with the studio underneath it, so the same company can also name, brand, and launch the business through our Launch practice when that is what the stage calls for.

The honest warning, and the junior-hire trap

Before product-market fit, do not hire one. A fractional CMO builds a system: positioning, channels, measurement. Built around the wrong customer, that system just gets you to the wrong answer faster and with more confidence. Early on you need discovery, not direction. The other common mistake is hiring a junior marketer full-time instead. A junior hire can run tasks. They cannot set strategy, judge channels, or tell a founder no — and they will learn marketing by spending your budget. A senior operator a few days a week costs less and decides better. Smaller companies face a version of the same choice, covered in fractional CMO for small business, and the timing signals are laid out in when to hire a fractional CMO.

Asked often.

How early is too early to hire a fractional CMO?

If you are still figuring out who the customer is and whether they will pay, it is too early. That stage needs customer discovery and cheap experiments, not a marketing system. Once a defined customer is buying and the problem is reaching more of them in an organized way, the seat starts paying for itself.

How many days a week does a startup need?

Most engagements run one to three days a week. A startup setting positioning and a first go-to-market plan usually needs one to two. More days make sense when there is a team to lead, agencies to manage, or a launch in motion. The cadence should follow the workload, not a package.

Can a fractional CMO help with fundraising?

Yes, in a specific way. Investors test whether the growth story and the numbers agree. A fractional CMO sharpens the narrative, builds the marketing metrics behind it, and closes the obvious gaps in the go-to-market plan before you are in the room. They cannot fix a story the numbers contradict.
