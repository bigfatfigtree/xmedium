What a Fractional CMO Actually Does All Week

It is not a part-time version of a marketing job. It is the whole job, pointed at fewer things, on the days that matter.

By Jeff Rimmer · November 14, 2026 · 5 min read

Most founders meet the fractional CMO idea at the same moment: marketing has become too important to improvise and too expensive to staff at the top. A full-time chief marketing officer is a serious executive seat, with a serious salary attached. Hiring a junior marketer and hoping they grow into the strategy is the other option, and it usually ends with a busy calendar and no direction. The fractional model sits between those two. A senior marketing executive embeds in your company part-time, owns the strategy, and runs the function as if the seat were theirs. Because it is, for the hours they are there.

Diagnosis before decoration

The first weeks are not campaigns. They are an audit. Where does revenue actually come from? Which channels produce customers and which produce reports? Where does money leak: the offer nobody understands, the landing page that loses people, the ad spend pointed at an audience that was never going to buy? A fractional CMO spends real time in the numbers before touching anything creative, because most marketing problems are not creativity problems. They are clarity problems wearing a creative costume. Until the diagnosis is done, every new idea is a guess, and guesses are the most expensive line item a growing company has.

One strategy everyone argues from

After diagnosis comes the document most companies do not have: a single strategy, written plainly, that every marketing decision gets tested against. Who we serve. What we say. What we will not do this year. This is the unglamorous core of the job, and it is the part that changes everything downstream. Once the strategy exists, the weekly work becomes enforcement. The founder stops re-deciding the direction every Monday. The team stops building three versions of everything. Good ideas that do not fit get killed, and killing them is not a failure of imagination. It is the job. A strategy that cannot say no is a list.

Running the calendar, managing the people

The middle of the week is operational. Launches and campaigns get planned on one calendar instead of five threads. Agencies, freelancers, and vendors get briefed properly, managed tightly, and held to outcomes instead of deliverables, so the founder does not have to do it between everything else. The fractional CMO sits in leadership meetings and translates: here is what marketing can do this quarter, here is what it cannot, here is what the numbers say we should stop funding. That translation, between the boardroom version of growth and the working version, is where a lot of companies quietly lose years. Someone senior has to stand in the middle of it.

How this differs from the alternatives

A consultant gives advice and leaves. The advice can be excellent, and then it sits in a document while the company goes back to improvising. An agency executes a brief. If the brief is wrong, the agency will execute the wrong thing beautifully, because execution is what you bought. A junior hire works hard and learns on your budget, but they need the strategy set for them, which means the founder becomes the CMO by default, at midnight, without the experience. The fractional CMO is the option that owns the outcome: embedded enough to be accountable, senior enough to set the strategy, and structured so the company pays for leadership hours instead of a full-time executive seat.

At Extra Medium, the practice is senior-only and founder-led, built on 20+ years across startups to Fortune 100 companies. It also sits inside an agency that builds, so the strategy and the execution live under one roof. The person who writes the plan is in the room when the plan meets reality, and the plan changes accordingly. That is the whole model: fewer hours than a full-time seat, none of them junior.
