The Room Got Small: What Happens to Celebrity Access When Fans Stop Watching and Start Showing Up

Hapsta is not another livestream. It is a ticket into a small live room where the celebrity talks with fans instead of at them, and it changes what a famous person is actually selling.

By Jeff Rimmer · November 14, 2026 · 4 min read

The livestream solved distribution and broke the encounter. A celebrity can reach a million people at once now, and every one of those people knows the celebrity cannot see them. Chat scrolls too fast to read. The comments are a crowd, not a conversation. Fans got infinite access to watch and almost no access to be in it.

Hapsta, a product from Extra Medium Labs and live now at hapsta.co, starts from the other end. A Hapsta session is a ticket to one live session with a famous person, online, on your phone. The room is capped on purpose. Most sessions are hangouts: a small live video room where the celebrity talks with the people in it. Fans can be seen, heard, and called on by name. It is built to feel less like a broadcast and more like being somewhere.

Small is the product

The tiers describe how present you are. Room gets you in, watching and talking in the room. Mic puts you in the talk line, able to speak when you are called on. Video puts your camera in the room, capped at 12 people, the closest thing to sitting across from someone. Nobody in the room is anonymous crowd. The cap is not a limitation the product apologizes for. The cap is the product.

Some sessions are game sessions instead of hangouts. The format is the same ticket, pointed at a different room: fans get a seat in the celebrity’s game party and play alongside them, while everyone else watches and talks from the room. The common thread is not the activity. It is the size. A capped room is a place a celebrity can actually host, and a place a fan can actually be noticed.

Watching is free everywhere now, and it feels like it. Being in the room is the part that got rare.

The celebrity sets the terms

Hapsta does not set prices, because the value of an hour varies too much from one name to the next. The celebrity sets the price, the length, and the room size, and keeps 80% of ticket revenue. One celebrity can run a small room at a high price. Another can run a bigger room cheap. Both are rational, and both are the celebrity’s call.

The work around the session is handled by an AI-assisted production board. It runs the talk line, the clock, the recording, and moderation prompts, so the person hosting does not need to produce their own show while starring in it. There is no streaming setup to buy and no gaming skill required. Talent applies to host; most have 20,000 or more followers somewhere, on any platform. What they bring is the audience and themselves. The platform brings the room and the machinery.

Access is being repriced

No payments are live on Hapsta yet. Fans join a waitlist and request the celebrities they want, and those requests decide who gets approached first. That order matters. Instead of booking whoever will say yes and hoping an audience follows, the platform can walk to a celebrity with a room already asking for them. The demand is documented before the pitch is made.

That is the shift worth watching. For a decade, celebrity access online has been sold by reach: how many people can this post hit. A capped room sells the opposite. It sells scarcity that is real, because a room with 12 cameras in it cannot be scaled without becoming the thing it replaced. The celebrity is not selling impressions. The celebrity is selling an hour of being known by name, and there is a hard limit on how much of that exists.

Whether fans keep paying for that is an open question, and the waitlist is how Hapsta finds out. But the direction is hard to argue with. Watching is free everywhere now, and it feels like it. Being in the room is the part that got rare.
