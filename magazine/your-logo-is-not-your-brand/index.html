Your Logo Is Not Your Brand

A logo is the label on the jar. Your brand is everything inside it, and everything about how the jar gets handed over.

By Jeff Rimmer · November 14, 2026 · 5 min read

Ask a founder what their brand is and most will point at the logo. Sometimes they’ll point at the color palette, or the typeface, or the mood board from the last redesign. All of those things are real, and all of them matter. But none of them is the brand.

Your brand is the set of expectations a customer carries into every interaction with you, and whether those expectations survive the interaction. It’s the work itself. It’s how fast you reply to an email, and whether the reply sounds like a person. It’s the invoice, clear or confusing. It’s the hold music, the packaging tape, the way a problem gets handled when something goes wrong at 4 p.m. on a Friday. The logo is just the label on the jar. It tells people which jar to reach for. It says nothing about what’s inside.

Why redesigns fail

This is why so many rebrands disappoint. A company changes the label and leaves the jar exactly as it was. The operations stay the same, the offer stays the same, the follow-through stays the same, and six months later someone is asking why the new identity didn’t move anything.

It couldn’t have. Customers don’t experience your identity system. They experience your behavior, in sequence, over time. If the old brand felt unreliable, that feeling wasn’t produced by the old typeface. It was produced by missed deadlines, inconsistent quality, and promises the business couldn’t keep at volume. A redesign that doesn’t touch any of that is a costume change. The market notices for about a week, then goes back to believing its own experience.

There’s a harder version of this problem, too. A sharper logo on an unchanged operation can actually make things worse, because it raises the expectation the operation then fails to meet. The gap between what you look like and what you act like is where trust goes to die.

Nobody ever left because of a typeface. They left because the second experience didn’t match the first promise.

What actually compounds

The brands that hold up over years are rarely the most designed ones. They’re the most consistent ones. A few things do the compounding:

Consistency. The same standard, kept in the tenth interaction as in the first. This sounds simple and is the hardest thing on this list, because consistency is an operations problem, not a design problem.

A point of view. A brand should stand for a specific way of seeing its category, stated plainly enough that a customer could repeat it. If your positioning can’t survive being paraphrased by a stranger, it isn’t finished.

Promises kept at scale. Every business keeps its promises when it’s small and the founder touches everything. The brand is what happens when the founder doesn’t. Systems, hiring, and standards are brand work, even though nobody puts them in the brand book.

None of this makes design unimportant. Design is how the point of view becomes visible, and visible matters. It just comes second in the order of operations, not first.

Fix this before anyone opens a design tool

When we run a launch or a rebrand, three things get settled before identity work starts, because identity built on unsettled ground has to be rebuilt later.

First, the offer. What exactly is being sold, to whom, and why is it the right choice for that person? If the team can’t answer in two sentences, the logo brief will quietly try to answer it instead, and logos are bad at that job.

Second, the story. Not a tagline, the actual account of why this business exists and what it changes for the customer. The story is what the identity has to carry. Decide the load before you design the vehicle.

Third, the proof. What can you point to? Work done, customers served, results you can state without decoration. A launch with proof can afford quiet design. A launch without it tends to ask design to shout, and shouting reads as doubt.

This is the order we build in on our Launch work: positioning, naming, identity, and go-to-market treated as one arc, not four separate purchases. And it’s the discipline behind our Reinvent work, too. A rebrand is a chance to fix the jar and the label in the right order. Most companies only fix the label because it’s the part you can see in a presentation. The jar is the part your customers can see.
