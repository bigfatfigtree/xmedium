The Local Advertiser Didn’t Leave. The Audience Did.

For decades a local business bought the paper, the radio station, and the TV spot because that was where the town was paying attention. The town is still paying attention. It is just looking at people now.

By Jeff Rimmer · November 14, 2026 · 5 min read

Where local attention used to live

There was a time when buying local media was a simple decision, because there were only a few shelves to buy from. The morning paper landed on every porch that mattered. The radio station played in every truck and kitchen. The evening news was a shared appointment. A restaurant, a car dealer, a furniture store: they bought those outlets not out of loyalty but out of arithmetic. That was where local attention lived, and attention is what advertising has always actually purchased.

That arithmetic held for decades, and then the audience moved. Not away from the town, and not away from local life. Away from the outlets. Attention went to the phone, and on the phone it did not settle on institutions. It settled on people.

The most trusted channel in town is a person

Ask a local business owner where their customers hear about things now and the answer is rarely a media brand. It is a person. The neighbor who posts where she eats. The dad who reviews every taco truck in the county. The creator with 3,000 followers, most of them within twenty miles, who actually shows up at the restaurant, eats the food, and posts about it to people who trust her because she is one of them.

That is not a lesser form of media. In practical terms it is a stronger one. The old local outlets delivered reach and a borrowed credibility. A local creator delivers both in a smaller, denser package: a defined local audience and a voice that audience chose to follow. The trust that used to attach to the station now attaches to the person, and it attaches more firmly, because the relationship feels like what it is: one person talking to people who know her.

The trust that used to attach to the station now attaches to the person.

A big market with no shelf

The scale of the shift is not in dispute. The creator economy is estimated at $250 billion, growing toward $480 billion by 2027. US creator ad spend is estimated at around $37 billion in 2025, growing roughly 26 percent a year. National brands have built whole departments around buying this attention.

Local businesses, by and large, have been locked out of it. Not because the value is unclear to them — they can see exactly who their customers listen to. They have been locked out because there was no shelf to buy it from. No standard way to find the local creators in their market, no way to see how much of a creator’s audience is actually local, no clean way to book, brief, and pay them. A national brand buys through platforms and agencies. A restaurant owner was left to slide into someone’s messages and hope. So the money mostly stayed in the old channels even as the audience drained out of them, or it went to boosted posts aimed at everyone and no one.

What we built about it

That gap is a building problem, so we built. Magpie is the platform our lab made for it: a place where a business can see a local creator’s actual local reach before booking them, not a follower count with a guess behind it, and then comp an experience or pay the creator’s listed rate, in the open, on standard terms.

The local advertiser never stopped wanting what they always wanted: the attention of the people nearby, delivered by a voice those people trust. For fifty years that voice belonged to an outlet. Now it belongs to a person. The businesses that figure that out first are not chasing a trend. They are just buying media where the town actually is.
